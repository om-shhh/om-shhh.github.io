> “While not all engineers are evil most of them are morally bankrupt because they’re smart enough to be absolutely obsessed with the minutiae of figuring out how things work and entirely too stupid to ask why or if that’s right in a moral context.”

Although I found this take to be somewhat misguided, it made me think about how my college engineering experience was molded by the institutional gravity of defense and big tech companies. I feel like so many people from my graduating class ended up working for defense companies like Lockheed Martin or Raytheon, and others ended up at tech companies led by greedy billionaire CEOs (including myself!). But why? How do seemingly cool and smart people avert their eyes from weapons of genocide and ecological destruction to justify employment? It can’t be as simple as individual moral bankruptcy. 

I think engineers are placed on an undeserved pedestal, labeled as intelligent, critical thinkers. Being good at technical reasoning does not translate to ethical reasoning. We take highly technical classes in physics and math, graded on finding solutions to problems within a constrained boundary, but we are never asked to question the constraint itself nor the context of the problem. Our coursework makes us analytically brilliant, but morally untrained because our engineering education structurally de-emphasizes ethics. The engineering ethics class fear-mongers students into obsessing over sound engineering design practices, selling the nightmare of a dresser falling on a baby while never questioning why companies at the career fair are shooting missiles at children. 

And why should they? Look around! These companies fund labs and design teams and research groups as multi-million dollar satellite offices[¹][²][³]. Students don’t just merely choose these careers individually, they are trained inside research ecosystems organized by defense funding. By the time a student encounters a job recruiter, their design team, advisor, and new classroom building are being funded by the Man himself. And going back to institutional gravity, we don’t get to choose from all possible careers, we choose from what visibly exists. The Palantirs and General Electrics of the world have the largest booths at the career fair, with clear job descriptions, known salary ranges, and social proof. Meanwhile, the public interest and community research orgs don’t appear in the pipeline at all. So as students we don’t weigh “genocide vs. justice” but “rent vs. unemployment.” 

Once we enter the industry, our performance is tied to shipping product, hitting milestones, and passing design reviews. We are not evaluated on asking why a product even exists or its geopolitical implications. To your manager, the question of consciousness is just a resource sink. We have no proximity to the consequences of our action, because our work is compartmentalized, and we use our learned engineering skills to obsess over sizing a bolt on a gas turbine that had its raw materials mined by children in the DRC, transported 57 times across oceans to have its steel processed, will emit 40 million metric tons of CO2, and end up in a landfill. But at least I can pay my rent. 

> “Ordinary participation in systems without felt malice”  
> — **Hannah Arendt**, *The Banality of Evil*

Our brains construct narratives to live with ourselves, and I would argue this isn’t ethically hollow, it’s our only way to justify living in a broken system that satiates the rich and starves everyone else. 

To be fully clear, I do not question the character of the individual; I question the system that makes certain career paths feel inevitable. In fact, for the individual I see no alternative; our young adult livelihoods depend on paying rent, chipping away at student loans, and relying on privatized healthcare. I also realize the subjectivity of my argument, in that people may actually align with the overall mission of the organization they work for. However, I contend that our engineering institutions failed us in providing meaningful spaces for serious ethical inquiry while they lined their pockets with blood money. We were robbed of cross-disciplinary training and exposure to community-scale engineering impact, and persuaded to virtue signal the big companies we were interning for each summer. 

And again to be unmistakably clear, this writing is not demanding engineers to simply refuse immoral work. That would be unrealistic and naive. We were, however, trained in systems that narrow our moral imagination.
