I recently read _Walking with the Comrades_ by Arundhati Roy which is a journalistic account of her lived experience with the Maoist (communist) movement in the forests of central India. The Maoists are a group fighting against the state and corporations for exploiting their Indigenous land for its raw materials, chopping down mountain tops for its bauxite and ore. In response, the Indian government has orchestrated Operation Green Hunt, a systematic extermination of tens of thousands of tribal peoples in these forests. Women have been raped, then killed; villages have been pillaged and burned. The Indian government has waged a war against its own people. There is extensive documentation, by Roy and other journalists, of mass displacement, extrajudicial killings, sexual violence, and criminalization of indigenous existence itself, enough to fit the UN’s textbook definition of genocide.

Before I bring forth the discomfort of my thoughts, I would like to be unmistakably clear: there is no ethical balance when one side has overwhelming coercive power and the other is rendered disposable. There is no place for human suffering of this scale in my moral arena. Given the industrialized and consumerist nature of our society, however, I question the level of comfort we’ve come to expect. Society has progressed technologically and our perceptions on quality of life have evolved. The questions I pose, as a member of the [global top 10%](https://wid.world/income-comparator/), is do modern societies need extraction to maintain comfort? Does this justify the destruction of ecosystems and annihilation of Indigenous communities? How much do I value my comfort? In the following parts of this writing, I would like to reframe these questions and offer my thoughts as a proposal for change.

Arundhati Roy’s accounts with the communist groups in India really helped me reframe my question of whether modern societies need extraction to maintain comfort. The real conflict is not “comfort versus forests”, it’s who gets to decide. Who defines necessity? And who pays for it? Middle-and upper-class comforts should not solely be defined by our material possessions, the electricity we consume, the new iPhones we purchase every year, or our public infrastructure. It is our comfort without proximity to consequence. The forests are far away. The Indigenous people are politically invisible, and violence is bureaucratized. I would argue our ethical failure isn’t consumption, but consumption without consent or accountability. Examples of this are found in every corner of the planet. From the construction of the [Mountain Valley Pipeline](https://www.selc.org/news/appalachia-cant-be-a-sacrifice-zone-for-an-irresponsible-dinosaur-like-the-mountain-valley-pipeline/), which will release 40 million tons of greenhouse gases into underserved Appalachian communities, to the [forced labor and persecution of Uyghur Muslims](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2025/05/29/world/asia/china-uyghur-xinjiang-labor-transfers.html) in China who are stitching the clothes on our backs, consumption without consent has ushered in a new era of oppression where both the consumer and the oppressed have [no voice in the consequences of our consumption](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.erss.2023.103406). Corporations and governments collude to weave intricate webs of legislation that effectively legalize the violence inflicted on these communities. This is violence without a legal perpetrator. There is no single person that “pulls the trigger”; harm is framed as an administrative outcome, not a moral choice. One of Roy’s resounding insights from the book was how land reclassification was used as a weapon. Forests that Indigenous people had lived on for generations were labeled as “state land” , “waste land”, or “resource-rich zones.” Once that land was legally reclassified, the removal of people became eviction, not displacement. Enforcement, not violence. A modern state can destroy whole ways of life while sincerely believing it is being reasonable, lawful, and progressive.

I am not a politician nor an economist; the nature of my proposals is not backed in research or community surveyorship. My proposals for justice are solely from the literature I read and the conversations I have. Nevertheless, I believe justice would require a radical transformation of resource governance, where communities have real veto power. They are not performatively consulted after decisions have already been made, and consent isn’t extracted through militarized action. Plans of extraction or growth should be co-authored and -signed by both the Indigenous peoples and developers. We must challenge conventional metrics of community development that prioritize economic growth, and instead investigate policy or technology interventions through a more holistic lens. De-growth and reduced consumption may be difficult but required in the global upper- and middle-classes. Our relationships with our hierarchy of needs, like food, water, and energy, must be reimagined and decentralized. Maybe if the consumption of our needs also affected the livelihoods of our neighbor, we would be more conscious of our decisions. Policy could be reframed to instead prioritize things like small-scale, community-controlled energy generation, permaculture design practices, and ecological governance by citizens of the community.

Most of us partake in societies that are predominantly capitalistic, an economic system that, by definition, rewards growth and profit at the hands of laborers. And to be honest, I don’t know what an ideal socioeconomic system would look like in a world plagued by issues like climate change, hunger, and poverty. Irregardless, I contend that comfort that requires someone else’s terror is not neutral, and “development” that needs permanent militarization is a lie. Although it is relevant and important to be incredibly conscious of our individual consumption, our deeper rethinking should not be to feel bad about the new phone we just purchased, but to question why a system is considered successful when it converts land into commodities and people into obstacles. A final thought and clarification, I am in no way romanticizing the Maoist (communist) guerilla movement in India, a group that has also committed violent atrocities against police officers and informants of the state. I am, however, accusing a system where violence becomes the only remaining language for people denied a political voice ([sound familiar?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Mangione)).
